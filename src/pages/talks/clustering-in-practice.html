Here's a simplified version of a system my team built recently. The company runs scripts on hotel websites and wanted to analyse the resulting data about (anonymised) website users, such as their rough location, room search history on the website, and what interest-specific pages they might have browsed. We wanted to use this to help our customers, who are mostly non-technical, get a broader idea of what kinds of people were searching and booking.

To achieve this, we need to look for groups and similarities in the data. One way of achieving this is using a technique called clustering.

Let's start with an example of some data points. To a human, it pretty obviously contains three main clusters. How do we get a machine to see them too?

We start by dropping three crosses (called centroids) completely at random.

For each point, we measure its distance to every centroid, and keep the nearest.

We then sum all points' distances to their nearest centroid. The goal of clustering is to minimise that total distance number.

We then move each cross to the average position (i.e. the centre) of the points it just claimed.

Then we repeat the measurement. We can see the distance has gone down a bit.

And now it's gone down a bit more.

We're done when the distance stops falling when we update the centroids.

mean (sensitive measure) medoid (resistant measure)

Clustering is a powerful technique, but it has a few gotchas we must be aware of. One such is the definition of 'average' in the sentence 'we move each cross to the average of its points'.

Two common definitions, seen here, are mean and medoid.

When we introduce a few large values to the dataset, the mean shifts but the medoid doesn't. Because of this behaviour, we call the mean a 'sensitive' measure and the medoid a 'resistant' one.

Adding a few outliers to the blue cluster produces the same behaviour: the sensitive mean (X) shifts more than the medoid (O).

A memory aid I use for this is: "Mean people are actually really sensitive."

Often, knowing which average to use comes down to the context of the problem. In this case, with booking value data, many guests didn't book (so their booking value is $0) but a handful did. We definitely want the sensitive measure, as we want the difference that those bookers make to the hotelier's bottom line to be reflected in the clusters.

total distance per k

Another thing I glossed over earlier was how we picked the number of clusters we want (which we call "k"). In that example we chose k=3, and there happened to be 3 clear clusters. How do you know the right number to choose when you can't eyeball the data?

This example illustrates how higher k always produces lower distance numbers, making higher k look naively like a "better" fit even when they are clearly not.

k = 3 · total 8,728
k = 7 · total 5,725

When we don't know how to choose k, one option is to do what's called a k-sweep. The optimum k will often be where the decrease in distance in between each increment stops decreasing quite as much. This is known as the knee and it's much easier to visualise than explain in words.

Going way past the knee means overfitting, which is a technical term for "not seeing the wood for the trees."

Fortunately, there are some standard checks for this. One such check is: what happens if you take a random sample of the points and rerun clustering on it? Do you have roughly the same groups or are the groups completely different?

Here's an example: we've refit clusters on not every single point but only a subset of them.

When we do this a few times, we can see on the left that the clusters roughly stayed in the same groups for the different subsamples, but on the right, the groups were shifting around a lot. This is known as cluster stability.

There's a numeric measure of this as well as a visual one, called the Jaccard index. (1 is perfectly stable and 0 is entirely unstable.)
This family of checks, where we re-run the algorithm with slight tweaks and see how much the result changes, is called bootstrapping.

Thanks for reading

This was an introduction to clustering. The main things to remember are:

  • Clustering is a powerful way to spot groups or commonalities in large amounts of data.
  • However, clustering always gives you an answer. It's on you to check the answer is meaningful, stable, and valuable in context.
